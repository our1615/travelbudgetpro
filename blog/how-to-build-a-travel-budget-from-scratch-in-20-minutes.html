How to Build a Travel Budget from Scratch in 20 Minutes

By Oliver Bennett · 2026-06-05 · 8 min read

You’ve booked the flights. You’ve told your boss you’re taking two weeks off. Now you’re staring at a blank notebook trying to figure out how much money to save. Sound familiar?

A woman named Priya called me last year, panicked. She had a trip to Italy booked in six weeks. She had no idea what anything cost in Rome or Florence. She’d saved £3,000 but had no clue if that was enough. She’d already spent £600 on flights. The rest was a mystery.

I walked her through the Travel Budget Calculator in fifteen minutes. She cancelled a planned hotel that would have blown her budget and found a cheaper guesthouse instead. She added a buffer for museum tickets (most were €15-25) and train transfers. By the end of the call, she had a realistic number: £2,400 for 12 days, including everything except flights. She was relieved. She’d been guessing £3,000, but that guess didn’t account for the daily espresso habit or the leather-shopping splurge she’d forgotten to plan.

Let me show you exactly how to build a travel budget from scratch in twenty minutes. No spreadsheets required. Just a phone, a calculator, and honesty about your spending habits.

Step one – pick your destination and trip length.

Priya knew she wanted 12 days in Italy: Rome (4), Florence (4), Venice (3), plus one travel day. She typed “Italy – moderate” into the calculator. The tool gave her a daily range of €80-120 per person. That’s £70-105. Multiplied by 12 days, that’s £840-1,260. That’s accommodation, food, local transport, and basic activities.

She was surprised by the low end. “I thought Italy was more expensive,” she said. I explained that the lower end assumes hostels, street food, and free attractions. The higher end assumes mid-range hotels, restaurant meals, and paid tours. She chose the middle – €100 per day – and moved on.

Step two – add your big-ticket items.

Flights, trains, and tours aren’t included in the daily estimate. The calculator has separate fields for those. Priya added:

Return flight from London to Rome: £180 (she already bought it)

High-speed train Rome→Florence→Venice→Rome: £120

Vatican tour (skip-the-line): £40

Uffizi Gallery ticket: £25

Doge’s Palace ticket: £30

Gondola ride (shared, not private): £25

Total big-ticket: £420. That’s separate from her daily budget.

Step three – add a buffer for “oops” moments.

Priya added 15% to her total. That gave her an extra £180. She didn’t think she’d need it, but she’d rather have it and not spend it than need it and not have it. Two weeks before her trip, her luggage broke. She used part of the buffer to buy a cheap carry-on. Then in Florence, she bought a leather jacket she hadn’t planned for. The buffer covered that too. She came back with £40 left.

Step four – decide on a tracking method.

Priya used a simple notes app on her phone. Every night before bed, she typed in what she’d spent. She categorised it: “food £12, transport £5, activity £15, other £8.” Total £40. She subtracted that from her remaining budget. It took ninety seconds.

A friend of mine, a guy named Tom, used a spreadsheet on his phone. He was more detailed – he tracked every coffee and gelato. That worked for him because he enjoyed the process. Priya didn’t. She just wanted to know if she was on track.

The Travel Budget Calculator doesn’t track spending for you – it sets the target. The estimator tracks progress.

Step five – adjust as you go.

Priya’s second day in Rome, she realised she was spending more on food than planned. Those €15 pasta lunches added up. She decided to have bigger breakfasts (included in her hotel) and lighter lunches (€5 panini). That saved her €10 a day. Over the trip, that’s €120 – enough for the gondola ride.

She also cut back on souvenirs. Instead of buying things for everyone, she bought one nice gift for her sister and sent postcards to others. Postage was €2 each. Much cheaper.

By day eight, she was ahead of her budget by €150. She used that to book a cooking class in Florence – €80 – and still had money left.

Priya considered adding a day trip to Pisa, but the tool showed her that the train plus entrance fees would add £60 to her budget. She decided to skip it. She didn’t regret that – she saw photos of the Leaning Tower online and felt fine.

One thing Priya wished she’d done: check the “holiday surcharge” box in the calculator. Italy has national holidays and local festivals where prices spike. She unknowingly booked her trip during a bank holiday weekend in Rome. Hotels were 20% higher. She didn’t realise until she arrived. Now the calculator has a date‑picker that warns you about local holidays.

She put £100 in “misc” for things like city taxes (Rome charges €6 per person per night), ATM fees, and bottled water. City taxes alone cost her €72 over 12 days. That’s £60. Without the misc budget, that money would have had to come from somewhere else.

So here’s the twenty‑minute plan, start to finish.

Minute 1-2: Open the Travel Budget Calculator. Enter your destination, trip length, and travel style (budget/moderate/luxury). Write down the daily range.

Minute 3-5: List your big‑ticket items – flights, long‑distance trains, expensive tours. Add them to the calculator.

Minute 6-7: Add a 15% buffer. If you’re travelling during peak season or to an expensive country, make it 20%.

Minute 8-10: Subtract your already‑paid costs from your total. That’s how much you still need to save.

Minute 11-15: Open the Trip Cost Estimator. Set your daily targets by category: food, transport, activities, misc.

Minute 16-18: Decide how you’ll track spending. Notes app? Spreadsheet? A receipt envelope? Pick one and commit.

Minute 19-20: Plan one “adjustment day” mid‑trip where you’ll review your spending and decide if you need to cut back or can splurge.

That’s it. Twenty minutes. No prior knowledge needed.

Priya’s trip cost breakdown after 12 days:

Daily spend (average): €92 – within her €100 target

Big‑ticket items: £420 as planned

Buffer used: £180 (luggage + leather jacket + cooking class)

City taxes, ATM fees, misc: £65 (covered by buffer)

Total: £1,085 (target was £1,240 – she came in under)

She spent the leftover £155 on a nice dinner on her last night and still had £40 left for souvenirs at the airport.

She told me “the best part was not stressing about money. I knew I could afford the gelato. I knew I could take that water taxi. I didn’t have to ask ‘can I afford this?’ every time.”

That’s the point. A budget isn’t a restriction. It’s permission to spend.

If you’re planning a trip, don’t guess. Don’t ask friends who have different spending habits. Use the Travel Budget Calculator. It’s free. It’s fast. And it will save you from that awful feeling of running out of money halfway through a trip.

Priya is already planning her next trip – Japan this time. She’s using the Travel Budget Calculator again, plus the Currency Budget Converter to handle yen fluctuations. She says “I’m a budget nerd now. I even helped my sister plan her honeymoon.”

You can be a budget nerd too. It only takes twenty minutes.
