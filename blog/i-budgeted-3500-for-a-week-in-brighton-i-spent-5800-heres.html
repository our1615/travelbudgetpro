I Budgeted 3500 For A Week In Brighton I Spent 5800 Heres

2026-06-24 · 3 min read · General
General

I Budgeted $3,500 for a Week in Brighton. I Spent $5,800. Here's Where the Money Went.

It was supposed to be the perfect trip. My wife and I had been planning it for six months. A week in Brighton, UK — my hometown, her first visit. The Royal Pavilion, the Lanes, the seafront, the pubs. I had budgeted $3,500. Flights from Denver to London Gatwick: $1,200. Airbnb in Kemptown: $800. Food and activities: $1,000. Miscellaneous: $500. I had it all mapped out in a spreadsheet. Color-coded. Formulas. Conditional formatting. I was ready.

I spent $5,800.

Not because I was reckless. Not because I went on a shopping spree. Because I had missed things. Things that don't show up in the "average cost of travel" blog posts. Things that the travel influencers don't mention because they're sponsored and their trips are free. Things that only show up when you're standing at a train station in London with a dead phone, a hungry wife, and a realization that the Gatwick Express costs £20 per person each way.

Here's the breakdown of the $2,300 I didn't budget for. The currency exchange fees: $180. I used my debit card at an ATM and got hit with a 3% foreign transaction fee plus a $5 ATM fee. Four times. The travel insurance I bought at the last minute because my wife got nervous: $120. The checked bag fees because our carry-ons were over the weight limit on the return flight: $140. The Uber from Gatwick to Brighton because the train was delayed and we were exhausted: $85. The extra night at the Airbnb because our flight was canceled due to a strike: $140. The emergency phone charger because mine died on day 3: $45. The pharmacy visit because my wife got a sinus infection: $80. The rain jackets because we didn't pack for British weather: $120. The list goes on.

I sat in a pub on our last night — The Cricketers, near the Royal Pavilion, where the ale is warm and the chips are thick — and I pulled out my phone. I opened my spreadsheet. I added a column. "Reality." I filled it in. Every unexpected expense. Every fee. Every "just this once" purchase. And I realized that my $3,500 budget was not wrong. It was incomplete. It was built on hope, not data. It assumed everything would go according to plan. And travel, by definition, never goes according to plan.

Here's what I learned. The rule of travel budgeting is not "estimate and hope." It's "estimate, then add 40%." Not because you're wasteful. Because you're human. Because flights get canceled. Because bags get overweight. Because sinus infections happen. Because British weather is British weather. And because the Gatwick Express is £20 per person, and nobody tells you that until you're standing at the ticket machine with a dead phone.

I rebuilt my spreadsheet when I got home. I added categories I had never considered. Currency exchange fees. Travel insurance. Baggage fees. Ground transport contingencies. Emergency accommodation. Medical expenses. Weather contingencies. Phone and charger replacements. I added a 40% buffer to every trip. Not because I want to spend more. Because I don't want to be surprised.

My next trip is to Barcelona in September. The spreadsheet says $4,200. The reality buffer says $5,880. I'm saving $6,000. Because I learned in Brighton that travel is not about the budget. It's about the buffer. And the buffer is what lets you enjoy the trip instead of panicking about the cost.

If you're planning a trip, do this. Build your ideal budget. Then add the categories you forgot. Then add 40%. Then save that amount. Because the worst part of travel is not the cost. It's the surprise. And the surprise is what turns a vacation into a stress-cation.

— Oliver, from a pub in Brighton where the ale is warm, the chips are thick, and the budget is now realistic
