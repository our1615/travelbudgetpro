Walking Through the Multi-City Trip Cost Tool: Europe, Asia, and Beyond

By Oliver Bennett · 2026-05-21 · 9 min read

The email came from a woman named Priya. She was planning a six-week trip to Southeast Asia: Thailand, Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos, Malaysia, Singapore. She’d never done a multi-country trip before. She had a spreadsheet with 12 tabs, but the numbers didn’t add up. Her flights were booked. Her rough itinerary was set. But she had no idea how much to budget for transport between cities, or how much more expensive Singapore would be than Chiang Mai.

I asked her to open the Multi-City Trip Cost Calculator.

The tool is designed for exactly this situation. You enter your cities in order, the number of nights in each, and your travel style. The calculator estimates accommodation, food, local transport, activities, and – critically – the cost of getting from city to city.

Priya’s itinerary: Bangkok (4 nights), Chiang Mai (3), Luang Prabang (2), Hanoi (4), Hoi An (2), Ho Chi Minh City (3), Phnom Penh (2), Siem Reap (4), Kuala Lumpur (3), Singapore (3). That’s 30 nights, plus travel days. She was travelling solo, mid-range – private rooms in guesthouses, mix of street food and casual restaurants, some paid tours.

The calculator returned a total of $2,800. That’s $93 per day. She was surprised. “I thought it would be cheaper,” she said. I explained that the transport between cities added about $400 – flights from Chiang Mai to Luang Prabang ($120), a train from Hanoi to Hoi An ($50), a bus from Phnom Penh to Siem Reap ($15), and flights to Kuala Lumpur ($80) and Singapore ($50). Plus a few ferries and tuk-tuks. Those costs add up.

The Travel Budget Calculator would have given her a single-country estimate, but the multi-city tool captures the connecting costs.

How to use the multi-city tool – step by step

Step 1: Enter your cities in order. Start with your arrival city. Add each subsequent city in the order you’ll visit them. The tool uses this order to calculate transport costs between consecutive cities.

Step 2: Enter the number of nights in each city. Full days count. The tool assumes your first night is the day you arrive, and your last night is the day before you depart.

Step 3: Select your travel style. Budget, mid-range, or luxury. This affects accommodation, food, activities, and the class of transport (economy flights, second-class trains, etc.).

Step 4: Review the breakdown. The tool shows you cost per city, transport between cities, and a running total. You can adjust each city’s daily spend up or down.

Step 5: Add flights, insurance, and buffer. The tool doesn’t include your international flights to the first city or from the last city. Add those manually. Same for travel insurance and an emergency buffer.

Priya’s international flights from London to Bangkok and Singapore to London cost her £700. Travel insurance £60. Buffer £200. Total trip cost: $2,800 + £960 ≈ £3,300. For six weeks – £550 per week. She was thrilled.

The hidden value of the transport estimator

Most travellers underestimate transport between cities. They think “a flight from Chiang Mai to Luang Prabang is short, so it must be cheap.” It’s not. The route is served by only one airline (Bangkok Airways) with a near-monopoly. Priya paid $120 for a 90-minute flight. The same distance in Europe would have cost $50.

The multi-city tool knew that. It pulled real data from flight and bus routes. It didn’t guess.

A traveller named Mark tried to do a similar route without the tool. He estimated $20 for the Chiang Mai–Luang Prabang flight. He was off by $100. He ended up cutting two days from his trip to afford the flight. He told me “I wish I’d known.”

Comparing cities within the tool

The tool also shows you city-by-city daily costs. Priya saw that Singapore was $120/day – more than double Chiang Mai ($50/day). She decided to cut Singapore from 3 nights to 2 nights and add a night in Kuala Lumpur instead. That saved her $70.

She also saw that Luang Prabang had cheaper accommodation ($15/night) than Hanoi ($25/night). She extended Luang Prabang by one night and cut Hanoi by one night. That saved her $10 and gave her an extra day in a place she loved.

Real-world examples: Multi-city routes and their costs

Route 1: Classic Europe (London – Paris – Amsterdam – Berlin – Prague – Vienna – Budapest). 21 days, mid-range. Estimated cost: £2,200 + flights. Transport between cities: £300 (trains, one flight). Daily average: £105. The tool’s transport estimate included seat reservations on the Eurostar (£40) and ÖBB night train (£50).

Route 2: Japan (Tokyo – Hakone – Kyoto – Osaka – Hiroshima). 14 days, mid-range. Estimated cost: £2,100 + flights. Transport between cities: £200 (Shinkansen, JR Pass equivalent). Daily average: £150. The tool’s JR Pass calculation showed that a 7-day pass was cheaper than individual tickets.

Route 3: USA road trip (San Francisco – Yosemite – Las Vegas – Grand Canyon – Los Angeles). 14 days, mid-range, renting a car. Estimated cost: £2,500 + flights. Transport between cities: £500 (car rental, gas, one one-way fee). Daily average: £180. The tool’s rental car estimate included insurance and mileage.

Route 4: South America (Rio – Iguazu – Buenos Aires – Santiago – Lima). 21 days, mid-range, flights between cities. Estimated cost: £2,800 + flights from home. Transport between cities: £600 (four flights). Daily average: £135.

What the tool doesn’t do

The Multi-City Trip Cost Calculator doesn’t book anything for you. It doesn’t check real-time availability. It doesn’t know if the train from Paris to Amsterdam is sold out on the day you want to travel. It gives you estimates, not guarantees.

But the estimates are based on real data. The flight estimates are from Skyscanner’s API. The train estimates are from Rail Europe and Deutsche Bahn. The bus estimates are from FlixBus and Omio. You can trust them to within 10-15%.

Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1: Not accounting for travel days. A travel day is not a full day of sightseeing. You’ll spend 3-6 hours moving between cities. The tool assumes you’ll spend half of your normal daily budget on travel days. Priya budgeted €15 for travel days – a meal and a coffee.

Mistake 2: Overestimating how many cities you can visit. The tool can help you see the cost of adding another city. Priya thought about adding a side trip to Koh Samui. The tool added $300 to her budget – flights, ferry, extra accommodation. She decided to skip it.

Mistake 3: Forgetting visa costs. Some countries require visas. Vietnam’s e‑visa is $25. Cambodia’s visa on arrival is $30. Laos is $40. The tool doesn’t include these. Priya added $100 for visas.

Mistake 4: Ignoring time of year. Prices for flights and accommodation vary wildly by season. The tool’s estimates are annual averages. If you’re travelling in peak season, add 20-30%. If you’re travelling in low season, subtract 10-20%. Priya travelled in shoulder season (May-June). Her actual costs were within 5% of the tool’s estimates.

Final advice for multi-city trips

Priya completed her six-week trip. She spent $2,950 – $150 over her $2,800 estimate. The overage came from a last‑minute flight change ($80) and more souvenirs than planned ($70). She said “I’m not upset. I had a buffer. The tool was close enough.”

She also said the multi-city tool saved her hours of research. “I would have spent days trying to figure out transport costs. The tool did it in 10 minutes.”

The Multi-City Trip Cost Calculator isn’t perfect. But it’s the best starting point for complex itineraries. It gives you a realistic number. It shows you where your money is going. And it helps you make trade-offs – add a city here, cut a night there, upgrade your transport here, downgrade your hotel there.

Use it before you book anything. Use it again after you book your flights. Use it one more time a week before you leave.

Priya used it three times. Her trip was stress‑free. Her budget held. And she came back with stories, not debt.
