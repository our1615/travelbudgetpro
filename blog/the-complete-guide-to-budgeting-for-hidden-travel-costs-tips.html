The Complete Guide to Budgeting for Hidden Travel Costs (Tips, Fees, Emergencies)

By Oliver Bennett · 2026-06-02 · 8 min read

Laura’s trip to Iceland was going perfectly until she returned her rental car. The agent pointed to a tiny scratch on the bumper – something Laura hadn’t even noticed. “That will be €900,” the agent said. Laura’s heart dropped. She had declined the rental company’s extra insurance because her credit card offered coverage. But her credit card required a police report for damage claims. She didn’t have one. She paid €900.

Hidden travel costs are the budget killers. They don’t show up in flight searches. They aren’t listed on hotel booking pages. They hit you at the rental car counter, on your final hotel bill, and in small print that nobody reads. I’ve seen trips go from “within budget” to “credit card debt” because of one unexpected fee.

Laura’s scratch wasn’t even her fault. The previous renter had left it. The rental company’s inspection process was sloppy. She could have avoided the whole mess by doing a video walkaround of the car before driving off – pointing the camera at every panel, every wheel, every mirror. She didn’t. That €900 mistake was expensive, but it taught her a lesson. Now she films every rental car for 60 seconds before leaving the lot.

Let me walk you through the hidden costs that catch most travellers – and how to budget for them.

Resort fees. In the United States, hotels add mandatory “resort fees” that aren’t included in the advertised room rate. These can be $25-50 per night. You don’t get a choice. If you see a hotel in Las Vegas for $59 a night, check the total with fees. It might be $109. The Travel Budget Calculator includes a toggle for resort fees, but only if you know to look. Always check “total with taxes and fees” before booking.

Tourist taxes. Many European cities charge a tourist tax per person per night. In Venice, it’s €1-5. In Paris, €2-8. In Rome, €3-7. It’s usually payable at the hotel, not included in your booking. For a two-week trip across five cities, that’s an extra €50-100. Budget for it.

ATM fees. International ATM withdrawals often incur a flat fee ($2-5) plus a percentage (1-3%). Some banks charge a “foreign transaction fee” on top. If you withdraw cash every three days on a month-long trip, you could pay $50 in fees alone. The solution: open a fee-free account with Wise, Revolut, or Starling before you travel. Withdraw larger amounts less often.

Currency conversion fees. When paying by card, some merchants offer to charge you in your home currency. This is called “dynamic currency conversion.” Always decline. The rate they offer is terrible – often 5-10% worse than the real exchange rate. Choose to pay in the local currency. The Currency Budget Converter can help you understand the real rate.

A friend of mine, a guy named James, was charged €40 extra on a €200 hotel bill because he accepted dynamic currency conversion at checkout. He didn’t notice until he got home. That’s a 20% markup. He was furious.

Cleaning fees on short-term rentals. Airbnb and Vrbo listings often show a low nightly rate and then add a $100 cleaning fee for a two‑night stay. That $80 room becomes $130. Always click through to the final price before comparing. Some platforms have a “total price” toggle – use it.

Resort parking. If you’re driving to a beach resort, check if parking is included. Many charge $20-40 per night for self-parking. Valet is even more. That’s an extra $100-200 on a week-long trip.

Tour booking fees. Online booking platforms add “service fees” of 5-15% to tours and activities. If you book a $100 snorkelling trip, the final price might be $115. If you book directly with the tour operator, you often avoid the fee. A traveller named Sarah saved €60 on a Greek island cruise by calling the boat company directly instead of using a third-party app.

Travel insurance deductibles. Many people buy travel insurance but forget that policies have deductibles – often $100-250 per claim. If you lose a $150 bag, the claim isn’t worth filing after the deductible. If your trip is cancelled and you lose $2,000, the deductible is fine. But for small claims, self-insure.

Medical evacuation costs. Most travel insurance policies have a limit for medical evacuation – often $100,000 or $250,000. That sounds like a lot. But a helicopter evacuation from a remote island can cost $50,000. A medically escorted flight from Asia to Europe can cost $150,000. If you’re travelling to remote areas, check your policy’s evacuation limit. The Travel Budget Calculator doesn’t include this, but you should.

Visa fees. Some countries charge visa on arrival fees that aren’t included in your flight or hotel budget. Cambodia charges $30 for a tourist visa. Egypt charges $25. India charges $25 for e-visa. These are small, but they add up. Budget an extra $50-100 per trip for visas.

Tip expectations. In the US, tipping is expected for restaurant meals (15-20%), hotel housekeeping ($2-5 per day), and tour guides (10-15%). In Japan, tipping is not expected and can be insulting. In Europe, rounding up is common but 10-15% is not required. Research your destination’s tipping culture before you go. An American couple in London left 20% on every meal – they spent an extra £200 they didn’t need to.

Flight change fees. Budget airlines like Ryanair and easyJet charge €45-65 to change a flight. If you miss your flight, you might have to buy a new ticket at full price. Build a €100 contingency per person for flight changes. I know a woman who missed her flight from Barcelona to London because of a delayed metro. She paid €180 for a new ticket. She hadn’t budgeted for it.

Exit taxes. Some countries charge departure taxes that are included in your ticket price – but not always. In the Philippines, you pay a domestic terminal fee (₱200) in cash at the airport. In Australia, the passenger movement charge is $60, but it’s usually included. Check before you go.

SIM card costs. Local SIM cards are cheap – often $10-20 for a month of data. But airport SIMs are expensive. Buy from a local convenience store instead. A traveller in Thailand paid 1,200 baht ($35) for a SIM at the airport. The same SIM cost 200 baht ($6) in a 7‑Eleven.

Laundry. If you’re travelling for more than a week, you’ll need to do laundry. Hotel laundry service is expensive – €5 per t-shirt, €10 for jeans. A laundromat costs €3-5 per load. Some hostels have free washing machines. Budget €10-20 per week for laundry.

Emergency medical deductible. Even with travel insurance, you may have to pay upfront for medical care and claim it back. A traveller in Costa Rica needed stitches after a zip‑lining accident. The clinic charged $400. She paid with her credit card and was reimbursed two months later. She had the cash flow to cover it. If you don’t, carry a credit card with a high enough limit.

Natural disaster contingency. A friend of mine was stuck in Bali when a volcano closed the airport for four days. His travel insurance covered extra accommodation and meals, but he had to pay upfront and claim later. The claim took eight weeks. He had to use savings to cover the €800. His “hidden cost” was the delay in reimbursement.

The best way to handle hidden costs is a contingency fund. I recommend 15% of your total budget. If your trip costs £2,000, set aside £300 for “unexpected.” Laura’s €900 rental car scratch would have been covered by a contingency fund – but she didn’t have one.

Laura now adds 20% to every budget she makes. She calls it the “stupid tax.” “If I do something stupid, or if something stupid happens, I pay for it from that fund. If nothing stupid happens, I buy myself something nice.”

That’s the right attitude.

Hidden costs won’t ruin your trip if you know about them. Research your destination. Read the fine print. Build a buffer. And never, ever skip the pre‑rental car video.
