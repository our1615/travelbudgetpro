How to Use the Japan Travel Cost Calculator for Your Exact Itinerary

By Oliver Bennett · 2026-06-03 · 5 min read

“How much should I budget for a week in Tokyo?” That’s the question I get more than any other about Japan. The answer is always the same: it depends on where you stay, what you eat, and how many temples you’re willing to pay for.

A reader named Emily emailed me last year with a specific itinerary. She and her husband had 14 days. They wanted to do the “golden route” – Tokyo, Hakone, Kyoto, Osaka, Hiroshima. She’d seen YouTube videos of people claiming they did Japan on $50 a day. She’d also seen luxury travel blogs quoting $500 a day. She was confused.

The Trip Cost Estimator is the tool she needed.

Emily plugged in her cities and the number of nights in each: Tokyo (4), Hakone (1), Kyoto (4), Osaka (3), Hiroshima (2). She selected “mid-range” as her travel style – private rooms in budget hotels, some convenience store meals, some restaurant meals, a mix of public transport and a 7-day JR Pass. The calculator gave her a total of $3,200 per person for 14 days, excluding international flights. That’s about $230 per day.

She was surprised. “I thought Japan was more expensive,” she said.

It can be. But the yen was weaker than usual, and she was willing to stay in business hotels (€60-80 per night) instead of luxury hotels (€200+). She also agreed to eat convenience store breakfasts – onigiri, yogurt, coffee – for €5 instead of hotel buffets for €25.

The Trip Cost Estimator helped her break down daily spending by category.

She allocated €60 per day for food (mix of convenience store, ramen shops, one nice sushi dinner), €30 for local transport (suica card), €20 for activities (temples, museums), and €10 for miscellaneous. Accommodation averaged €70 per night. The JR Pass cost €250 for 7 days. Total: €3,200.

One thing the calculator doesn’t automatically include is the Hakone Free Pass – a 2‑day pass for the Hakone loop that costs ¥5,000 (€35). Emily added it manually. She also added a budget for a ryokan stay in Hakone – a traditional inn with kaiseki dinner and onsen. That cost €250 for one night, including dinner and breakfast. It was her splurge. She said it was worth every yen.

Emily also learned about the “luggage forwarding” service – takkyubin. She sent her large suitcase from Tokyo to Kyoto for €15. That saved her from dragging it through Hakone’s mountain trains. She added that as a line item in her miscellaneous budget.

A mistake she almost made: buying the JR Pass for the wrong duration. She planned 14 days, with travel on days 1, 5, 7, 9, 11, and 14. A 7‑day pass would cover days 5-11. The calculator showed her that a 14‑day pass would cost twice as much and wouldn’t pay off. She bought the 7‑day pass and used suica for the rest.

Another traveller I know, a guy named Ben, didn’t use the calculator. He showed up in Tokyo with no budget, no JR Pass plan, and no idea how expensive taxis are (very). He spent €80 on a taxi from Narita Airport to his hotel – the train would have cost €15. He ate at expensive restaurants every night. He paid for individual shinkansen tickets instead of a pass. His 10‑day trip cost €4,500. He told me “I wish I’d done five minutes of research.”

Emily’s trip went smoothly. She tracked her spending in a notes app and checked it against the Trip Cost Estimator every few days. She ended up spending €3,450 – €250 over budget. The overage came from a few things: a kimono rental in Kyoto (€50), a tea ceremony (€40), and more souvenirs than planned (€160). She didn’t regret any of it.

She told me “I could have done it cheaper. I could have stayed in hostels, eaten only konbini food, skipped the tea ceremony. But I didn’t want to. The calculator gave me a realistic number, and I stuck close to it.”

Her top budget tips for Japan:

Get a Suica or Pasmo card for local trains and convenience store purchases. Reload it with ¥5,000 at the start.

Buy your JR Pass before you leave – it’s cheaper than buying it in Japan.

Don’t take taxis unless you have to. The subway is excellent.

Eat breakfast from 7‑Eleven or FamilyMart. The onigiri is fresh and costs ¥150.

Book your ryokan months in advance. The best ones sell out.

Use luggage forwarding. It’s cheap and efficient.

The Trip Cost Estimator also includes a currency converter. Emily used it to check the yen‑pound rate daily. The rate was stable during her trip, so no surprises.

She’s already planning a second trip – this time to Hokkaido in winter. She’s using the same tool to budget for skiing, snow festivals, and onsen. Her target is €3,000 for 12 days. She thinks it’s doable.

If you’re going to Japan, don’t guess. Plug your itinerary into the Trip Cost Estimator. It’s free. It’s accurate. And it will save you from Ben’s mistake.
