Digital Nomad Economics: Cost of Living, Co-Working, and Tax Implications by City

By Oliver Bennett · 2026-05-30 · 7 min read

Sophie’s monthly spending in Lisbon was €1,400. Her monthly income from UK clients was €4,000. She was saving €2,600 a month – more than she’d saved in a year living in London. But her accountant called her with bad news: she might owe UK tax on that income, even though she was living in Portugal. And Portugal’s NHR (Non-Habitual Resident) scheme was changing.

Sophie’s story is common. Digital nomads chase lower costs and better weather, but they often ignore the tax implications. The savings from cheaper rent can be wiped out by an unexpected tax bill.

I ran Sophie’s numbers through the planner. Lisbon vs London: Lisbon was 42% cheaper. But after tax, the gap narrowed. She paid 40% income tax in the UK. Under Portugal’s NHR scheme, she paid a flat 20% on her professional income. That made the move worthwhile. But NHR was ending for new applicants in 2025. Sophie got in just before the cutoff.

A friend of mine, a freelance writer named Jamie, moved to Bali in 2023. He didn’t research the visa. He arrived on a tourist visa, then overstayed by two weeks. He was fined, banned from re‑entry for six months, and had to scramble to work from Malaysia instead. The cost of that mistake: $1,200 in flights, $500 in fines, and two weeks of lost productivity.

Cost of living by city (monthly, solo, mid‑range)

These numbers come from real nomads who shared their budgets. They include rent, food, local transport, co‑working, health insurance, and a small buffer for fun.

Chiang Mai, Thailand – $1,000. Rent for a nice studio: $300. Food: $200 (street food and market cooking). Co‑working: $100. Scooter rental: $80. Health insurance: $50. Miscellaneous: $270. The nomad hub of the world for a reason.

Medellín, Colombia – $1,100. Rent in El Poblado: $500 (higher than other areas, but safer). Food: $200. Co‑working: $150. Transport: $40. Health insurance: $60. Miscellaneous: $150. The weather is perfect year‑round.

Lisbon, Portugal – $1,800. Rent in a central area: $900. Food: $400. Co‑working: $150. Transport: $50. Health insurance: $100. Miscellaneous: $200. Not cheap anymore, but cheaper than London.

Bangkok, Thailand – $1,200. Rent near BTS: $400. Food: $250 (more if you eat Western). Co‑working: $120. Transport: $60. Health insurance: $50. Miscellaneous: $320. The city never sleeps, and neither does your Wi‑Fi.

Mexico City, Mexico – $1,300. Rent in Condesa or Roma: $600. Food: $300. Co‑working: $150. Transport: $30. Health insurance: $70. Miscellaneous: $150. Great food, great culture, reliable internet.

Barcelona, Spain – $2,200. Rent: $1,000 for a small apartment. Food: $500. Co‑working: $200. Transport: $60. Health insurance: $100. Miscellaneous: $340. The digital nomad visa here requires proof of income (€2,000+ per month).

Bali (Canggu), Indonesia – $1,300. Rent in a private villa: $600. Food: $250. Co‑working: $150. Scooter: $80. Health insurance: $50. Miscellaneous: $170. The internet is better than you think.

Cape Town, South Africa – $1,400. Rent in a safe area: $500. Food: $350. Co‑working: $120. Transport: $100 (Ubers). Health insurance: $70. Miscellaneous: $260. Load shedding (power cuts) is an issue, but many co‑working spaces have backup generators.

Tax implications for digital nomads

Tax is the hardest part of nomad life. Here’s a simplified guide. I am not a tax professional. This is not advice. But here’s what I’ve learned from talking to accountants.

Most countries tax you if you stay more than 183 days in a year (the “substantial presence” test). Some countries tax you from day one if you work remotely for a foreign company. Others only tax income earned within their borders.

Portugal’s NHR scheme (now closed to new applicants) offered a flat 20% tax rate for ten years. Spain’s “Beckham Law” allows a flat 24% for six years. Italy has a 70% tax reduction for new residents in southern regions. Greece offers a 50% reduction for seven years.

The catch? You often need to become a tax resident. That means staying more than 183 days. That means applying for a visa. That means paperwork.

A nomad named Sarah spent three months in Spain, three months in Portugal, and three months in France. She stayed less than 183 days in each country. She remained a tax resident of her home country (the UK). She paid UK tax on her worldwide income. Simple.

Her friend Tom stayed 200 days in Spain. He became a Spanish tax resident. He was required to pay Spanish income tax on his worldwide income, even though his clients were in the US. He didn’t know. He got a letter from the Spanish tax agency two years later demanding €18,000.

Co‑working costs and internet reliability

The planner includes co‑working costs, but the real factor is internet. In Chiang Mai, the internet is fast and cheap. In Bali, it’s fast but expensive. In Lisbon, it’s fine. In Cape Town, load shedding can cut your connection.

I have a rule: before committing to a nomad city, book one month of accommodation with a refundable cancellation. Work from cafes and co‑working spaces for that month. Test the internet at different times of day. If it fails, move.

A nomad named Alex tested five cities in six months. He used the planner to compare costs. He ended up in Kuala Lumpur – not the cheapest, but the internet was the most reliable. He told me “I’d rather pay $1,500 a month for fast internet than $1,000 for constant frustration.”

Hidden nomad costs

Health insurance with nomad coverage. Normal travel insurance doesn’t cover long‑term stays. You need a plan designed for nomads – SafetyWing, Genki, World Nomads. These cost $50-150 per month.

Visa runs. If you’re on a tourist visa, you may need to leave the country every 30-90 days. Flights, buses, and border crossing fees add up. A nomad in Colombia flew to Panama every three months – $300 each trip.

Local SIM cards. Easy and cheap in most countries. But if you need to keep your home number for 2FA codes, you might pay roaming fees. Use an eSIM service like Airalo for backup.

Coworking memberships. Monthly passes cost $100-200. Daily passes cost $10-15. If you work five days a week, the monthly pass is cheaper.

Equipment replacement. Laptops break. Headphones get lost. Phones get stolen. Budget $50-100 per month for gear replacement.

Sophie’s final advice for aspiring nomads: “Don’t quit your job and move on a whim. Test it first. Take two weeks of remote work from a coffee shop in your own city. Then take a month in a cheap nomad hub. Then decide.”

She’s been in Lisbon for two years now. She’s saved over €50,000. She’s dating a Portuguese chef. She’s learning the language. She has no plans to move back.

“The tax stuff was scary, but I found a good accountant. The cost of living was lower than I expected. The loneliness was harder than I expected. But I joined a running club, then a book club, then a dinner club. Now I have more friends here than I did in London.”
