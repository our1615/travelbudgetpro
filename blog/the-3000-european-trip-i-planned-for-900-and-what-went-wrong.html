The £3,000 European Trip I Planned for £900 and What Went Wrong

I sat in my flat in Brighton at 2 AM, staring at a spreadsheet that said £903.47, and I felt like a genius. I had planned a two-week trip to Europe — Barcelona, Rome, Prague, Berlin — for under £1,000. Flights: £87 with Ryanair. Accommodation: £240 for 12 nights in hostels. Food: £180 at £15 per day. Transport: £120 for trains and buses. Activities: £120 for museums, walking tours, and the occasional beer. Miscellaneous: £156.47 for emergencies, souvenirs, and the inevitable "I forgot to budget for this."

I am a budget travel blogger. I have been writing about cheap travel for five years. I have backpacked through Southeast Asia on £20 a day. I have slept in airports, eaten street food, and hitchhiked across countries. And I was about to learn that Europe in July is not Southeast Asia. And that £15 per day for food in Rome is not a budget. It is a fantasy.

It started in Barcelona. The hostel was £20 per night — fine. But it was August. It was 35 degrees. The hostel had no air conditioning. I slept on the roof. The roof was hard. The roof was noisy. The roof had pigeons. I did not sleep. I was exhausted. I was cranky. And I was hungry.

I had budgeted £15 per day for food. In Barcelona, a coffee and a croissant cost £4. A sandwich for lunch cost £6. Dinner — even a cheap dinner of tapas and a beer — cost £12. That was £22 per day. Not £15. And that was without dessert. Without snacks. Without the gelato I bought because it was 35 degrees and I was miserable. On day three, I spent £28 on food. I had blown my food budget by 87%.

I tried to compensate. I skipped breakfast. I bought bread and cheese from the supermarket. I ate one meal a day. I told myself this was "intermittent fasting" and not "poverty." It did not work. By day five, I was dizzy. I was irritable. I was standing in the Sagrada Familia, one of the most beautiful buildings in the world, and I was thinking about how much a Big Mac cost.

Then the train to Rome was delayed. Six hours. I missed my hostel check-in. They gave away my bed. I had to find a new hostel at 11 PM. The only one with space was £35 per night — 75% over budget. I stayed two nights. That was £70 instead of £40. Another £30 gone.

In Rome, I made a new mistake. I had budgeted £10 per day for activities. The Colosseum was £16. The Vatican was £17. The Borghese Gallery was £15. That was £48 for three activities. I had budgeted £30 for three days. I was already £18 over. And I had not done a walking tour. I had not gone to a museum. I had not seen the Pantheon. I had not done any of the things I had planned to do.

I started cutting. I walked instead of taking the metro. I ate supermarket sandwiches instead of restaurant meals. I skipped the activities I had dreamed about. I stood outside the Colosseum and looked at it through the fence. I told myself I would come back. I knew I would not.

By Prague, I was exhausted. Not just physically. Emotionally. I had planned this trip for six months. I had saved £900. I had told everyone I was going to Europe for under £1,000. And I was failing. I was sitting in a hostel common room, eating instant noodles, while other travelers went out to dinner. I was avoiding conversations because I could not afford to go to the bar. I was lonely. I was miserable. And I was only halfway through.

I called my sister from Prague. I told her I was thinking about coming home early. She said, "You have been planning this for six months. You saved £900. You are in Europe. Do not come home." I said, "I am miserable." She said, "Then spend more money." I said, "I do not have more money." She said, "Then borrow from me. Pay me back later. But do not waste this trip because you are too proud to admit your budget was wrong."

She sent me £300. I did not want to take it. I took it. And I spent it. I ate real food. I went to the museums. I took the walking tours. I had the beers. I stayed in a decent hostel with air conditioning. I saw the Colosseum from the inside. I saw the Vatican. I saw the Pantheon. I saw Prague Castle. I saw Berlin. And I came home with £12 left and a credit card balance of £180.

The total cost of the trip was £1,368. Not £900. Not £1,000. £1,368. That was 52% over budget. And it was worth every penny of the extra £468. Because the alternative was standing outside the Colosseum, looking through the fence, telling myself I would come back.

I learned something I should have already known: budget travel is not about suffering. It is about being realistic. £15 per day for food in Europe in summer is not realistic. £20 per night for a hostel in August is not realistic. £10 per day for activities in major cities is not realistic. And pretending otherwise does not make you a good budget traveler. It makes you a hungry, miserable person who misses the point of travel.

I still budget. I still plan. I still look for deals. But I add 40% to every budget now. Because things go wrong. Because trains are delayed. Because hostels lose your booking. Because you need gelato when it is 35 degrees. Because travel is not a math problem. It is an experience. And experiences cost money.

— Oliver, from Brighton, where the spreadsheets are realistic and the gelato is finally budgeted.
