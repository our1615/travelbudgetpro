Airbnb vs. Hotel vs. Hostel: A Complete Cost Analysis for Every Travel Style

By Oliver Bennett · 2026-05-26 · 8 min read

The Airbnb cleaning fee was $150. For a two-night stay. The hotel down the street was $120 per night, all taxes included. The hostel was $40 per night, including breakfast. The traveller, a woman named Megan, booked the Airbnb without checking the total price. She paid $150 for cleaning on a $200 booking. That’s a 75% surcharge.

Megan’s mistake is common. She saw the nightly rate of $100 and thought “that’s a deal.” She didn’t click through to the final price. By the time she added cleaning and service fees, the Airbnb was more expensive than the hotel. And the hotel had a pool, free breakfast, and daily housekeeping.

A friend of mine, a solo traveller named James, swears by hostels. He’s 45. He doesn’t care about the bunk bed. He cares about the social aspect and the price. He told me “I’ve met people in hostels that I’ve travelled with for months. You don’t get that in a hotel.” He pays $25-40 per night in Europe, $15-25 in Southeast Asia. His annual accommodation budget is $3,000. He stays in hostels 80% of the time.

Another friend, a couple named Sarah and Tom, prefer hotels. They value privacy and quiet. They pay $120-200 per night in Western Europe, $80-120 in Eastern Europe, $50-80 in Southeast Asia. They told me “we’d rather work an extra week than share a bathroom with strangers.”

A family of four, the Wilsons, use Airbnbs for long stays (more than a week) and hotels for short stays. They learned that Airbnbs with high cleaning fees are terrible for short stays but reasonable for long stays. A $150 cleaning fee on a 2-night stay adds $75 per night. On a 10-night stay, it adds $15 per night – much more reasonable.

The hidden costs of each option

Airbnb. Cleaning fees, service fees, security deposits, late check-in fees, linen fees, “extra guest” fees. What looks like a $70 room becomes $110 after fees. Also, some Airbnbs require you to do chores before leaving – strip the beds, take out the trash, start the dishwasher. That’s fine for a week-long stay. For an overnight stop, it’s a pain.

Hotel. Resort fees (in the US), parking fees (city hotels), breakfast not included (budget hotels), Wi-Fi fees (some older hotels). The total price you see on a booking site is often not the final price. Click through to the payment screen. A hotel that shows $100 on the search results might be $130 after taxes and fees.

Hostel. Locker rental (some hostels charge for lockers), towel rental (€2-5), laundry (€5-10), breakfast (€5-10, sometimes included). The base price is low. The add-ons are small but add up. A hostel that shows $20 might end up costing $30 with locker, towel, and breakfast.

I used it to compare accommodation in five European capitals. London: hotel $180, hostel $40, Airbnb $120. Paris: hotel $160, hostel $35, Airbnb $100. Berlin: hotel $100, hostel $25, Airbnb $70. Rome: hotel $130, hostel $30, Airbnb $90. Amsterdam: hotel $170, hostel $40, Airbnb $110. For a 2-week trip visiting all five, the cheapest option was hostels ($410 total), then Airbnbs ($980), then hotels ($1,460). That’s a $1,000 difference.

When to choose each option

Choose Airbnb when: You’re staying more than 5 nights. You need a kitchen. You’re travelling with a group of 4+ people. You want a local experience. You don’t mind doing some chores. The cleaning fee is reasonable relative to the length of stay.

Choose hotel when: You’re staying 1-4 nights. You want daily housekeeping. You need a predictable experience. You have luggage you don’t want to carry up stairs. You’re travelling for business. You want a pool or gym.

Choose hostel when: You’re a solo traveller. You want to meet people. You don’t mind shared spaces. You’re on a tight budget. You’re travelling light. You’re under 35 (though older travellers are welcome).

The middle ground

There are options between these three. Guesthouses are like budget hotels with local character. Pensionen in Germany, pensión in Spain, ryokan in Japan. They cost between hostel and hotel prices. They often include breakfast.

Bed and breakfasts (B&Bs) are another option. They cost hotel prices but include a home-cooked breakfast. They’re good for couples on a romantic trip.

Apartment hotels (like Citadines or Adina) combine the space of an Airbnb with the service of a hotel. They have a kitchenette, a living area, and daily housekeeping. They cost more than Airbnbs but less than hotels for stays of a week or more.

A traveller named Emma stayed in an apartment hotel in Kuala Lumpur for $60 per night. She had a kitchen, a washing machine, and a pool. An Airbnb would have been $50 plus fees. A hotel would have been $80. She chose the middle option and was happy.

The psychological cost

Not every cost is monetary. There’s a psychological cost to each option.

Airbnb requires communication with the host. You need to coordinate check-in, ask for the Wi‑Fi password, figure out the lockbox. For introverts, that’s draining. For extroverts, it’s fine.

Hotel is anonymous. You check in, you get a key, you leave. No small talk. No chores. For tired travellers, that’s a relief.

Hostel requires social energy. You share a room with strangers. You might snore. Someone else might snore. You have to lock up your valuables. For social travellers, that’s a feature, not a bug.

A traveller named Hannah told me “I love hostels. But after a month, I need a hotel room with a door I can close. The social battery runs out.”

Final advice

Use the Accommodation Cost Comparator before every booking. Don’t assume one option is always cheapest. A hotel can be cheaper than an Airbnb for a short stay. A hostel can be cheaper than both. A guesthouse can be the sweet spot.

Book refundable rates if there’s any chance your plans might change. The extra $5-10 per night is insurance.

Read the reviews. Look for mentions of noise, cleanliness, and bed bugs. A cheap room with bed bugs is not cheap.

And remember: you’re not just paying for a bed. You’re paying for a good night’s sleep. Don’t skimp to the point where you wake up tired and grumpy. A good night’s sleep is worth $20.
