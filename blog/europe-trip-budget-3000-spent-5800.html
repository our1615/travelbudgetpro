Europe Trip Budget 3000 Spent 5800

2026-07-06 · 5 min read · General
General

The spreadsheet was beautiful. I had color-coded categories, currency conversion formulas, and a contingency line item of 15% — which I thought was generous. I was going to backpack through Europe for three weeks on $3,000. That was $142 per day, which seemed reasonable for hostels, trains, and the occasional nice dinner. I was wrong about everything. The spreadsheet lied. Europe was expensive. And I came home with $5,800 on my credit card and a new understanding of what "budget travel" actually means.

I'm Alex Chen. I run travelbudgetpro.org from San Francisco, California, where the rent is high and the travel dreams are even higher. I built this site because I was tired of travel bloggers who claimed to do Europe on $50 a day while staying in sponsored hotels. This story is the honest version.

The trip started in London, which was mistake number one. London in 2026 is not a budget destination. My hostel bed — a bunk in a room with twelve other people, one of whom snored like a chainsaw — was $45 per night. My Oyster card for the Tube was $8 per day. My first meal, a fish and chips from a pub near Victoria Station, was $18. And that was before I converted pounds to dollars, which added 20% to everything. My $142 daily budget was gone by lunch.

Paris was next. The Eurostar train was $89. My Airbnb in Montmartre was $120 per night, which I had budgeted for, but the cleaning fee was $65 and the "service fee" was $42. That's $227 for a room that smelled like cigarettes and had a shower the size of a phone booth. I walked everywhere to save money, which meant I was eating 4,000 calories a day and still losing weight because French portions are small and my feet were doing marathon mileage.

The food was where the budget really died. I had planned $30 per day for meals. In reality, breakfast was $8 (croissant and coffee), lunch was $15 (a sandwich that wouldn't satisfy a child), and dinner was $35 if I wanted to sit down anywhere that didn't have a clown as a mascot. And I wanted to sit down. I was walking ten miles a day. I deserved a chair. The $30 budget became $58, and that was on days I skipped dessert.

The hidden costs were the real killers. ATM fees: $5 per withdrawal, plus 3% foreign transaction fees. I withdrew cash six times. That's $30 in fees, plus $45 in transaction costs. My phone plan didn't include international data, so I bought a local SIM card for $25, then ran out of data in week two and had to buy another. The museums I wanted to see — the Louvre, the Rijksmuseum, the Uffizi — were $20-$25 each. I had budgeted for three museums. I went to nine.

And then there was the stuff I didn't budget for at all. A rain jacket in Amsterdam because I forgot mine and it poured for three days. $65. A replacement phone charger because I left mine on a train. $25. A doctor visit in Rome for a stomach bug that I blame on a questionable gelato. $120, because I didn't have travel insurance. A last-minute train ticket from Florence to Rome because I missed my booked one after sleeping through my alarm. $85. These "emergencies" totaled $295. My contingency fund was $450. I burned through it in ten days.

By week three, I stopped checking my budget. I knew I was over. I knew the number would make me sick. So I just enjoyed the trip and dealt with the consequences when I got home. I ate the pasta in Bologna. I drank the wine in Tuscany. I took the gondola in Venice, which was $90 for twenty minutes and worth every ridiculous second. I was $2,800 over budget, but I was also in Venice, and the spreadsheet couldn't see the sunset.

The 2026 travel landscape has made budget travel harder, not easier. Inflation has hit Europe hard. The euro is strong against the dollar. Budget airlines have added so many fees that a $20 flight becomes $80 by the time you add a bag and a seat. And the "budget" hostels of 2015 are now "boutique" hostels charging $50 a night for a bunk. The dream of cheap Europe is dead. The reality is expensive, but still worth it.

I built the tools on this site because I needed them. The trip budget planner forces you to use real numbers, not fantasy ones. The daily spending tracker keeps you honest in the moment. The currency converter with fees shows you what things actually cost, not what the exchange rate says. These tools won't make Europe cheap. But they will make you honest about what you're spending.

If you're planning a Europe trip, double your budget. Then add 20%. That's the real number. And if you can't afford it, save longer. Don't do what I did — don't come home to a credit card bill that makes you question every gelato you ate. The gelato was worth it. The stress wasn't.

What's your real Europe budget — not the spreadsheet version, but the honest one?
