Using the Digital Nomad Budget Planner to Compare 5 Cities Side by Side

By Oliver Bennett · 2026-05-20 · 8 min read

The email arrived at 7 AM on a Monday. The subject line: “Help me decide.” A reader named Chloe was in the middle of a quiet crisis. She was a freelance graphic designer living in London. Her rent was £1,600 a month. Her studio was £400. Between taxes, utilities, and the Tube, she was spending nearly £3,000 a month just to exist. She had savings – about £15,000 – but they were draining faster than she could refill them. She wanted to leave. She didn’t know where to go.

She’d narrowed it down to five cities: Lisbon, Barcelona, Chiang Mai, Medellín, and Cape Town. She’d read blog posts about each. She’d watched YouTube videos. But the numbers were all over the place. One blog said Lisbon cost €1,200 a month. Another said €2,000. She didn’t know who to trust.

I asked her to open the Digital Nomad Budget Planner.

The tool allows you to compare up to five cities side by side. You enter your estimated monthly spending for each category, or you trust the tool’s defaults. The defaults are based on real data from nomads who’ve lived in those cities.

Chloe ran the comparison.

Lisbon, Portugal. Estimated monthly total: €1,700. Breakdown: rent €900 (one-bedroom apartment in a central area), food €350, co-working €150, transport €50, health insurance €100, miscellaneous €150. That’s about £1,450. Compared to her £3,000 in London, she’d save £1,550 a month.

Barcelona, Spain. Estimated monthly total: €2,000. Rent €1,100, food €400, co-working €200, transport €60, health insurance €100, miscellaneous €140. That’s about £1,700. Still cheaper than London, but not by as much.

Chiang Mai, Thailand. Estimated monthly total: $1,100. Rent $400 (nice studio in Nimman), food $250, co-working $100, transport $50 (scooter rental), health insurance $50, miscellaneous $250. That’s about £850. A £2,150 monthly saving compared to London. The number shocked her.

Medellín, Colombia. Estimated monthly total: $1,200. Rent $500 (Poblado), food $250, co-working $150, transport $40, health insurance $60, miscellaneous $200. About £950. She could live on less than a third of her London burn rate.

Cape Town, South Africa. Estimated monthly total: $1,400. Rent $600 (safe area, furnished), food $350, co-working $120, transport $80 (Ubers), health insurance $70, miscellaneous $180. About £1,100. More expensive than Chiang Mai, but still far cheaper than London.

The hidden factors the tool doesn’t show

Chloe was excited by the numbers. But I reminded her that cost of living isn’t the only factor.

Time zone. London to Chiang Mai is 6 hours ahead. She’d be working UK hours, which means starting work at 2 PM and finishing at 10 PM. That’s fine if she’s a night owl. Not fine if she’s not. Medellín is 6 hours behind London. She’d be working from 3 AM to 11 AM. That’s brutal. Lisbon and Barcelona are in the same time zone as London. Cape Town is 1 hour ahead.

Visa rules. Thailand offers a 60-day tourist visa, extendable for another 30 days. After that, she’d have to do a visa run – fly to another country and come back. Some nomads do this every 2-3 months. It adds cost and hassle. Portugal offers a D7 visa for remote workers. Spain has a digital nomad visa requiring proof of income (€2,000+ per month). Colombia has a 90-day tourist visa extendable for another 90 days. South Africa has a 90-day tourist visa with no extension; after that, she’d have to leave.

Internet reliability. Chiang Mai has excellent internet. Medellín has good internet but occasional outages. Cape Town has load shedding (power cuts) that can knock out internet for hours. Chloe’s work requires reliable video calls. She couldn’t afford random outages. That ruled out Cape Town.

Safety. Medellín is safe if you stay in the right neighborhoods. Cape Town has safety concerns. Chiang Mai is very safe. Lisbon and Barcelona are generally safe but have pickpocketing.

Community. Chloe didn’t want to be alone. Chiang Mai has a huge nomad community. Lisbon’s nomad scene is growing but less established. Barcelona’s is small. Medellín’s is active but younger.

She set alerts for the Thai baht, Colombian peso, South African rand, euro, and British pound. The tool notified her when the baht was weak against the pound. She exchanged £500 at a good rate and saved about £30.

How to use the nomad planner for a real decision

Chloe narrowed her list to two: Chiang Mai and Lisbon. She ran the nomad planner again, this time adjusting the defaults to match her actual lifestyle. She didn’t drink alcohol – so her “miscellaneous” could be lower. She didn’t need a co-working space every day – she could work from home half the time. She wanted a private one-bedroom apartment, not a room in a shared house.

Chiang Mai adjusted: rent $350, food $200, co-working $50 (10 days a month), transport $30, health insurance $50, miscellaneous $100. Total $780. That’s about £600.

Lisbon adjusted: rent €800, food €280, co-working €80, transport €40, health insurance €100, miscellaneous €120. Total €1,420. That’s about £1,200.

The difference was £600 a month. Over a year, that’s £7,200. That’s a lot of money. But Chloe valued being closer to her family in the UK. Lisbon was a 2-hour flight to London. Chiang Mai was 15 hours plus a layover. She chose Lisbon.

She moved in May. Her rent was €850 for a one‑bedroom in Estrela. Her internet was fast. She found a co-working space for €120 a month. She joined a nomad WhatsApp group and made friends. She told me “I could have saved more in Thailand, but I would have been lonely. The planner helped me see the trade-offs.”

Common mistakes with the nomad planner

Mistake 1: Using the defaults without adjustment. The defaults are based on average nomads. If you drink alcohol, add €100. If you eat out every meal, add €200. If you need a private office, add €300.

Mistake 2: Forgetting startup costs. Chloe forgot to budget for a new laptop (€1,500), a noise-cancelling headset (€200), and a backup power bank (€50). The planner doesn’t include these. She added them to her “relocation budget.”

Mistake 3: Ignoring taxes. If you stay more than 183 days in a country, you may become a tax resident. Chloe planned to stay 180 days in Portugal, then go back to the UK for the rest of the year. She avoided Portuguese income tax. The planner doesn’t calculate taxes. She had to research this herself.

Mistake 4: Underestimating health insurance. Chloe’s travel insurance didn’t cover long-term stays. She needed nomad-specific insurance (SafetyWing, Genki). That cost $50 a month. The planner’s default was $60 – close enough.

Real user story: Comparing five cities for a developer

A reader named Sam was a software developer making £70k a year remotely. He ran the planner for five cities: Lisbon, Barcelona, Tallinn, Prague, and Buenos Aires. He adjusted the numbers for his lifestyle – he wanted a two‑bedroom apartment (for an office), high‑speed internet, and a good co-working space.

The planner showed him:

Lisbon: €2,200/month

Barcelona: €2,400/month

Tallinn: €1,800/month

Prague: €1,600/month

Buenos Aires: $1,300/month

He chose Prague. He liked the architecture, the central location in Europe, and the low cost. He moved in April. He told me “the planner gave me confidence. I knew I wasn’t making a mistake.”

Final advice for using the nomad planner

The Digital Nomad Budget Planner is a tool for comparison, not a guarantee. It gives you a ballpark. Your actual spending will be different. But the differences between cities are large enough that the ballpark matters.

If you’re considering the nomad life:

Use the planner to compare at least three cities.

Adjust the defaults to match your actual lifestyle.

Research visa rules and time zones before you commit.

Add a 20% buffer for startup costs and emergencies.

Visit your top choice for a month before moving permanently.

Chloe is still in Lisbon. She’s been there six months. She’s saving £1,200 a month compared to London. She’s happy. She told me “the planner gave me the push I needed.”

The planner won’t make you brave. But it will make you informed. And informed is close enough.
