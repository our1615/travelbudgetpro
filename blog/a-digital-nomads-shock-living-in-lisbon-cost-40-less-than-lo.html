A Digital Nomad's Shock: Living in Lisbon Cost 40% Less Than London

By Oliver Bennett · 2026-06-08 · 8 min read

The WhatsApp message came through at 11 PM. A friend named Sophie, who’d been a corporate lawyer in London for eight years, had just moved to Lisbon. She’d sold her car, sublet her flat in Clapham, and packed two suitcases. She was done with the 6 AM alarms, the overpriced Pret sandwiches, and the £1,800-a-month rent for a one-bedroom flat with mould in the bathroom.

Her To startweek in Lisbon, she sent me a voice note. “Oliver, I’m paying €850 for a two-bedroom apartment with a balcony and a view of the river. My coworking space is €120 a month. A coffee is €1.20. A glass of wine is €2.50. I went out for dinner last night – three courses, wine, espresso – and it cost €28. I would have paid £85 for the same meal in London.”

She wasn’t exaggerating. The Digital Nomad Budget Planner confirmed it.

I asked her to run a comparison: London vs Lisbon, same lifestyle – eating out three times a week, renting a one-bedroom flat, using public transport, working from a coworking space. The tool showed a 42% difference. Lisbon was cheaper in every category except one: flights back to the UK. But she only needed to fly once every three months for client meetings. The savings on rent alone covered the flights.

Sophie wasn’t a “digital nomad” in the Instagram sense. She wasn’t working from beachside cafes or posting photos of her laptop with a coconut. She was a serious professional who’d discovered that her income – which she earned remotely from UK-based clients – went twice as far in Portugal.

She wasn’t alone. A guy I know named Alex, a software developer from Manchester, moved to Chiang Mai last year. He told me his monthly expenses dropped from £2,500 to £900. He still works for the same company. His boss doesn’t care where he is as long as the code gets written. Alex is saving £1,600 a month. That’s £19,200 a year. He’s using it to buy a house back in the UK – something he never thought possible.

Sophie used the travel budget calculator To start just to estimate a three-month trial in Lisbon. When the numbers showed she could live on €1,400 a month while earning £4,000, she booked her flight. The nomad planner gave her the confidence to stay longer.

One of the biggest surprises for her was healthcare. She assumed she’d need expensive international private insurance. Then she discovered that as a legal resident (she got a D7 visa, she could access Portugal’s public healthcare system. She pays €70 a month in social security contributions and gets comprehensive coverage. Her private insurance in London had cost £120 a month with a £1,000 excess.

He told me about the time he missed the last bus from Chiang Mai to Pai. The station was closing. A local woman selling grilled pork skewers noticed him standing there with his backpack, looking at the timetable for the third time. She walked over, pointed at a phone number scribbled on a piece of cardboard, and said something in Thai he did not understand. He called the number. A man with a pickup truck showed up twenty minutes later. The ride cost him 300 baht — more than the bus, but he got to Pai by midnight. He sat in the back with three crates of live chickens. The smell was unforgettable. He told me this story while we were eating breakfast in a Brighton café, and I could still see the chickens in his eyes.

The thing about travelling on twelve dollars a day is that every decision becomes a math problem. Do you walk forty minutes in the heat to save the thirty-baht tuk-tuk fare? Do you skip the air-conditioned café and drink your coffee standing at a street stall? Do you sleep in the dorm with the broken fan because it is two dollars cheaper? Tom made these decisions every hour of every day. He had a small notebook where he wrote down every expense. Not on his phone — a physical notebook, with a pen. He said the act of writing made him feel the cost. A digital entry felt like nothing. A handwritten number felt like a promise.

Another surprise was taxes. Portugal’s Non-Habitual Resident (NHRscheme offered a flat 20% income tax rate on certain professions for ten years. Sophie’s UK tax rate had been 40%. She hired a local accountant for €200 to sort it out. That one-time fee saved her thousands.

Sophie used it to plan a route from Lisbon to Barcelona to Paris to Berlin. She wanted to test other cities before committing to Portugal long-term. The calculator showed that Barcelona was only slightly more expensive than Lisbon for rent, but food cost 30% more. Paris was double. Berlin was cheaper than Paris but more expensive than Lisbon. She stuck with Lisbon.

The hidden cost of nomad life that nobody talks about? Loneliness. Sophie told me that her To startthree months were tough. She didn’t know anyone. She ate dinner alone most nights. She joined a Facebook group for digital nomads in Lisbon, went to two meetups, and met a small circle of friends. She said “you have to budget for socialising. Not just money – time. You have to actively make friends. They won’t appear at your door.”

Another nomad I know, a guy named Dan, made the opposite mistake. He moved to Bali because he saw influencers posting about “paradise.” He didn’t research the visa rules, the internet stability, or the healthcare. He arrived, rented a villa for $800 a month, and then discovered that the WiFi was unreliable for video calls. He had to buy a Starlink dish – $600. Then his tourist visa ran out after 60 days, and he had to fly to Singapore for a visa run – $400. He lasted four months before moving to Thailand.

The Nomad Budget Planner includes a visa cost estimator now, because of stories like Dan’s. It also includes a “reliability factor” for internet – green, yellow, red. Dan’s Bali villa was yellow. He needed green.

Sophie’s advice for anyone thinking about the nomad life is simple. “Don’t quit your job. Test it first. Take two weeks of remote work from a coffee shop in your own city. If you can handle the distractions, book a one-month trial somewhere cheap. If you survive that, you’re ready.”

She also recommends keeping a “return fund” – three months of living expenses back in your home country. That way, if you hate nomad life or your situation changes, you can go home without panic.

Sophie has been in Lisbon for 14 months now. She’s not sure if she’ll stay forever, but she’s not leaving anytime soon. She told me “I used to spend £1,800 a month just to exist in London. Now I spend €1,400 and actually enjoy my life. I go to the beach after work. I eat fresh seafood for dinner. I sleep eight hours. My stress is gone.”

She still pays UK taxes on her income. She still has a British bank account. She still votes. But her feet are in Portugal, and her budget thanks her every single day.

If you’re a remote worker feeling squeezed by your city’s cost of living, run the Nomad Budget Planner. Compare your city to three others. You might be surprised.
